## P37: Make labels identify the actual action, provider, and system meaning

Use labels that describe the real action or provider; check current provider
guidance. A label and its supporting line should let users predict the next
screen, including an outcome they may not expect, such as an advertisement or a
survey, and, when the effort is fixed, how much it takes. Entry copy in a
notification, link preview or banner should indicate what kind of content it
opens, and the landing screen should continue that promise and offer the action
the entry was used for; concealing the destination to provoke curiosity works
against this. Review copy by reading each screen as a whole and the flow in
order, checking for breaks and repetition, not only sentence by sentence.

## P41: Review retention and exit friction against the user’s intended goal

Assess exit friction against the user goal, not retention alone. Before keeping
a device that steers a choice, check that declining or leaving is easy, that
the steering is visible and accurately worded, and that there is evidence the
steered outcome serves the person rather than only a metric; report a missing
condition as a finding without asserting intent. For a lock or limit people
place on their own money or resources, ask whether the person keeps a way to
reach what is theirs, whether the effort to release it varies with ability,
and whether release is framed as failure or guilt. The release conditions
themselves, including any approval the person chose to require, stay with the
policy owner. Check an accessible alternative before recommending a hold or
timed gesture.

## P55: Check defaults for consent and costly-to-reverse choices in context

Do not preselect a choice that amounts to consent, such as turning on a data
backup the person did not ask for. For a choice that is costly to undo when
committed by mistake, such as a payment plan, a value most users pick overall
can still fail a segment, customer or context; check that before keeping it as
the default, and where needs vary, consider letting the configuring party
require an explicit choice instead. An unselected required choice needs a
visible prompt and an error that names what is missing. Consent wording must
describe what is actually authorized, not a convenience it resembles. Which
defaults and consent texts are acceptable is the policy or legal owner's
decision; propose the change with its evidence rather than setting it.
